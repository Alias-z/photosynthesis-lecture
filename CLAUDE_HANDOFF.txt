Photosynthesis

Photo: capturing solar energy (45 minutes)

Pigments give photosynthetic organisms their colours and absorb light. The light reactions convert that absorbed energy into ATP and NADPH, which supply energy and reducing power for carbon assimilation.

Sunlight supplies the energy; CO₂ supplies the carbon. The two lectures explain how photosynthesis brings them together.

SLIDE 2: Why is the sky blue, and why are leaves green?

CONTEXT (CONFIRMED 22 OCTOBER 2026)
Why is the sky blue, and why are leaves green?

Different interactions with the same sunlight

The blue sky and a green leaf illustrate two different ways that matter changes the light reaching our eyes. Sunlight contains a broad range of wavelengths. The colour we perceive depends on which wavelengths reach us, their relative intensities, and our visual sensitivity.

Why air makes the sky appear blue

Sunlight’s oscillating electric field induces oscillations in the charges within air molecules. These produce radiation in other directions: light is scattered. For molecules much smaller than the wavelength, Rayleigh scattering applies, with scattering strength approximately proportional to 1/λ⁴.

Consequently, 450 nm blue light is scattered about 4.4 times more strongly than 650 nm red light under the same conditions. When we look away from the Sun, some of this scattered sunlight reaches our eyes, enriched in shorter wavelengths. Rayleigh scattering changes the light’s direction while essentially preserving its wavelength. It does not convert red photons into blue photons.

https://acd-ext.gsfc.nasa.gov/anonftp/acd/daac_ozone/Lecture4/Text/Lecture_4/rayleigh.html

Blue rather than violet, and the different colours of sunset

Violet scatters even more strongly, but perceived colour depends on the entire scattered spectrum and the eye’s response. Our lower sensitivity to violet helps explain the blue appearance.

At sunset, sunlight travels through a longer atmospheric path. More short-wavelength light is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving the transmitted sunlight relatively enriched in red and orange. The observer’s viewing direction therefore matters: scattered skylight and the direct solar beam have different spectra.

https://science.nasa.gov/ems/03_behaviors/

https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/

Why a leaf appears green

Chlorophyll’s molecular structure gives it wavelength-dependent absorption bands, with strong absorption in the blue and red regions and weaker absorption in much of the green region. Absorption transfers photon energy into an electronic excitation of the pigment. The surrounding protein also influences these absorption properties.

https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay2058

Pigment chemistry and tissue structure

A whole leaf combines this selective absorption with reflection, transmission and internal scattering. Light changes direction at interfaces within the tissue, including those between hydrated cell walls and air spaces. Some light escapes back towards the observer. Because pigments remove blue and red light more strongly, the escaping visible light is relatively enriched in green. Leaf colour therefore reflects both pigment chemistry and tissue structure.

https://doi.org/10.2307/2657019

A green appearance does not mean most green light is rejected

A leaf can absorb substantial green light while reflecting more green than blue or red. Weaker absorption also allows green light to penetrate deeper before absorption. Under strong white light, this can deliver additional excitation to deeper chloroplasts whose photosynthetic capacity is less saturated than that of chloroplasts near the illuminated surface.

Terashima and colleagues demonstrated this advantage for additional green light in sunflower leaves; it depends on the background illumination.

https://doi.org/10.1093/pcp/pcp034

SLIDE 3: The solar spectrum

CONTEXT (CONFIRMED 22 OCTOBER 2026)
The solar spectrum

Sunlight and human vision

Sunlight supplies both energy and information to life. It contains a broad range of wavelengths; a prism separates its visible components into a continuous spectrum from violet to red. Human eyes detect roughly 380–780 nm, using this light to recognise objects, colours and movement. This is the human visible range.

https://science.nasa.gov/ems/03_behaviors/

https://cie.co.at/eilvterm/17-21-003

Photosynthetically active radiation

Plants capture energy from an overlapping part of the spectrum. Photosynthetically active radiation—PAR—is conventionally defined as 400–700 nm, encompassing blue, green and red light. Photosynthetic pigments absorb photons within this range to drive the light reactions. PAR gives us a common basis for measuring light available for photosynthesis; the amount actually absorbed depends on the pigments and the leaf.

https://cie.co.at/eilvterm/17-26-079

https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay2058

Far-red light beyond conventional PAR

Biological activity continues beyond these boundaries. Far-red photons around 700–750 nm can support photosynthesis alongside shorter-wavelength light, illustrating why conventional PAR is a measurement range rather than a strict limit on photosynthesis.

https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.13730

Ultraviolet, infrared and microwaves

Toward shorter wavelengths, ultraviolet can trigger protective responses and damage cells at excessive exposure. Toward longer wavelengths, absorbed infrared contributes to warming tissues. Microwaves lie farther beyond infrared; their absorption by water and other molecules provides the familiar example of heating food. Different parts of the spectrum therefore interact with living material in different ways.

https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-arplant-050718-095946

https://science.nasa.gov/ems/07_infraredwaves/

https://gml.noaa.gov/outreach/info_activities/pdfs/TBI_electromagnetic_radiation.pdf

SLIDE 4: Photon energy and scattering

CONTEXT (CONFIRMED 22 OCTOBER 2026)
Photon energy and scattering

Photon energy

Light carries energy in discrete packets called photons. Einstein’s 1905 light-quantum proposal connects each photon’s energy to its frequency: E = hν = hc/λ, where h is Planck’s constant, c is the speed of light and λ is wavelength. Shorter wavelengths therefore carry more energy per photon: a blue photon carries more than a red photon. Increasing light intensity at a fixed wavelength supplies more photons without changing each photon’s energy. This distinction matters when comparing the energy supplied by different colours.

https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/the-dual-nature-of-light-as-reflected-in-the-nobel-archives/

Rayleigh scattering

Wavelength also affects how light is scattered. Light’s electric field makes charges within air molecules oscillate, producing radiation in other directions. Rayleigh scattering increases approximately as 1/λ⁴, so blue light is scattered much more strongly than red. This wavelength dependence comes from the molecules’ electromagnetic response; greater photon energy alone does not explain it. Scattering changes the photon’s direction while essentially preserving its wavelength.

https://psg.gsfc.nasa.gov/helpatm.php

The blue sky and sunset

When we look away from the Sun, this scattered light reaches our eyes and gives the sky its blue appearance. At sunset, sunlight travels through more atmosphere. More blue light is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving the sunlight reaching us relatively enriched in red and orange.

https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/

CONTEXT (CONFIRMED 22 OCTOBER 2026)

Why are leaves green?

Selective absorption and leaf colour

Light reaching a leaf can be absorbed, reflected or transmitted. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light strongly, but green light more weakly. Consequently, the reflected light is relatively enriched in green, giving leaves their familiar colour. Green appearance does not mean that most green light is reflected.

https://doi.org/10.1093/pcp/pcp034

Different pigments, different absorption spectra

The pigments have different absorption spectra. Chlorophyll a and b have shifted blue and red absorption peaks, so their contributions overlap without being identical. Carotenoids absorb mainly in the blue to blue-green region, extending light capture. Peak positions also depend on the pigment’s molecular environment.

https://doi.org/10.1038/nchembio.1555

Green light inside a leaf

Green light still supports photosynthesis. Its weaker absorption lets it penetrate deeper into the leaf, while internal scattering lengthens its path and increases opportunities for absorption. Under strong white light, additional green light can reach deeper chloroplasts whose photosynthesis is less saturated, making it more effective than additional red light. Leaf anatomy and pigment distribution matter; thickness alone does not guarantee this advantage.

https://doi.org/10.1093/pcp/pcp034

Accessory pigments in algae

Other photosynthetic organisms use different pigment combinations. In the red alga Griffithsia pacifica, phycoerythrin absorbs strongly around 540 and 565 nm, capturing green wavelengths that chlorophyll absorbs relatively weakly. These wavelengths are within conventional PAR: the contrast is between pigment absorption spectra.

https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24278

SLIDE 6: Light-harvesting complexes

CONTEXT (CONFIRMED 22 OCTOBER 2026)

Light-harvesting complexes

Why chlorophyll a and b absorb differently

Chlorophyll a and b absorb different wavelengths because their molecular structures differ. Chlorophyll b has a formyl group (–CHO) where a has a methyl group (–CH₃). This changes the electron distribution and the energy gaps that photons must match. Surrounding protein charges also affect these gaps, so the same pigment can absorb differently in different protein environments.

https://doi.org/10.1039/D1CP02580E

Complementary absorption and different roles

Their absorption spectra complement each other. Chlorophyll b broadens light collection and generally transfers excitation to lower-energy chlorophyll a states. In land plants, chlorophyll a also supplies the principal donor pigments for reaction-centre photochemistry.

https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay2058

Organisation enables excitation transfer

To make these pigments work together, proteins organise them into light-harvesting complexes. The proteins control pigment spacing, orientation and excitation energies, allowing rapid transfer towards a reaction centre. During antenna transfer, excitation moves between pigments; there is no net electron transfer between them. Electron transfer and charge separation occur when the reaction centre performs photochemistry.

https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay2058

LHCII and LHCI in land plants

LHCII mainly supplies photosystem II and usually forms a trimer of three proteins. Each protein binds 8 chlorophyll a, 6 chlorophyll b and 4 carotenoids. Its chlorophyll a:b ratio is approximately 1.3.

LHCI supplies photosystem I and usually consists of four proteins beside the PSI core. The whole belt in one pea structure contains 45 chlorophyll a, 12 chlorophyll b and 13 carotenoids. Its chlorophyll a:b ratio is approximately 3.7–3.8.

LHCII is called the major antenna because its numerous copies bind a large fraction of plant chlorophyll. PSI also contains a substantial chlorophyll-a-rich core antenna.

https://doi.org/10.1093/plphys/kiac175

What the pigment ratios imply

The pigment ratios have functional consequences. LHCII’s greater proportion of chlorophyll b strengthens complementary absorption. LHCI contains proportionally more a, including specially coupled pairs that create low-energy “red forms” absorbing beyond 700 nm. These arise from pigment arrangement and protein interactions, not from the ratio alone. Some require thermally assisted uphill transfer to reach the reaction centre: extending spectral capture can therefore slow trapping.

https://doi.org/10.1007/s11120-013-9838-x

Two experiments, two discoveries

Two experiments connect this organisation to its discovery. Emerson and Arnold’s 1932 flash experiments helped establish that many chlorophyll molecules cooperate within a photosynthetic unit. Emerson enhancement in 1957 showed that red and far-red light together could produce more photosynthesis than the sum of their separate effects. The first supports collective light harvesting; the second helped reveal two cooperating light reactions, leading into how PSII and PSI drive electron transport together.

https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz171
