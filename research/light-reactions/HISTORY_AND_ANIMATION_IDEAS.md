# Historical experiments and possible animations

Research checked 21 October 2026. Ordered by the lecture sequence, not by date. **Animation ideas below are proposals only.** Dates refer to the original work; later digitization dates are not discovery dates.

## 1. Why the sky is blue: Strutt/Rayleigh, 1871

**Story.** The paper was signed J. W. Strutt, later Lord Rayleigh. He derived the strong wavelength dependence of scattering by sufficiently small particles: intensity scales as λ⁻⁴. His introduction explicitly discusses Tyndall's particle-cloud experiments. The familiar modern explanation involving air molecules should not be attributed in its entirety to this one early paper.

**Possible animation.** Send the same mixed beam through a short and a long path. Show preferential blue scattering to the side and progressive reddening of the transmitted beam.

**Accuracy check.** A colloid demonstration is a particle-scattering analogy. Keep wavelength-dependent scattering distinct from the photon-energy relation E = hc/λ. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786447108640452).

## 2. Packets of energy: Planck, 1900; Einstein, 1905

**Story.** Planck introduced energy elements in his treatment of thermal radiation. Einstein extended the argument to light quanta and the photoelectric law. These are distinct steps in the history of E = hν. Einstein's 1921 Nobel Prize, awarded in 1922, specifically cited the photoelectric law.

**Possible animation.** Compare photon number and photon energy using separate controls: increasing brightness adds photons; shortening wavelength increases energy per photon.

**Accuracy check.** Photoelectric emission from a metal is different from excitation of a chlorophyll molecule. Do not animate an electron leaving every antenna pigment. [Einstein's paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19053220607), [Nobel archive](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/the-dual-nature-of-light-as-reflected-in-the-nobel-archives/), [Planck's 1900 work](https://educationalgames.nobelprize.org/educational/physics/quantised_world/energy-4.html).

## 3. Taking the green out of a leaf: Pelletier and Caventou, 1817/1818

**Story.** Two Paris pharmacists isolated and studied green plant material and introduced the name chlorophyll. A 2024 historical paper translates their original accounts, including treatment of plant pulp with alcohol. This is a concrete chemistry story, long before a chlorophyll molecular structure or reaction centre was known.

**Possible animation.** Move from leaf to extract to separated pigment bands, then to absorption spectra. Mark the chromatography step as a later explanatory extension, not their original experiment.

**Accuracy check.** Their green extract was not a complete modern identification of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and all accessory pigments. [Translations and historical context](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11120-024-01081-x).

## 4. Bacteria as oxygen detectors: Engelmann, 1882

**Story.** Engelmann used oxygen-sensitive bacterial behaviour to connect illumination wavelength with oxygen production by an alga. It is an early action-spectrum experiment: the response of photosynthesis, rather than pigment absorption alone.

**Possible animation.** Spread a spectrum along an algal filament; allow bacteria to gather where oxygen production is greatest.

**Accuracy check.** Bacteria indicate oxygen, not a preference for coloured light. Do not turn the red/blue peaks into a claim of zero green-light photosynthesis. [Historical synthesis, “Early observations”](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz171), [contemporary 1882 account of the oxygen test](https://www.nature.com/articles/025327a0).

## 5. Light coming out in a different colour: Stokes, 1852

**Story.** Stokes systematically studied wavelength-changing emission and named fluorescence. His paper acknowledges earlier observations by Herschel and Brewster, and examines quinine and alcoholic leaf-green solutions. He should not be portrayed as the first person ever to notice glowing plant extracts.

**Possible animation.** Absorption, rapid internal relaxation, then red fluorescence from a chlorophyll extract. Introduce competing pathways when moving to the intact leaf.

**Accuracy check.** Fluorescence is newly emitted light; it is not green light merely reflected or scattered. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstl.1852.0022), [Royal Society biographical record](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?id=NA8283&src=CalmView.Persons).

## 6. A leaf's glow changes after switching on the light: Kautsky and Hirsch, 1931

**Story.** Fluorescence from a dark-adapted leaf changes after illumination, even with steady incident light. This linked a visible signal to changing photosynthetic processes. A 2025 historical note provides a commented English version of the 1931 report and explains why Kautsky's singlet-oxygen interpretation was mistaken.

**Possible animation.** Keep the lamp steady while the leaf's fluorescence trace rises and falls; later reveal reaction-centre closure and regulatory processes.

**Accuracy check.** Distinguish the original induction observation from today's saturation-pulse/PAM methods. A correct observation can outlive its initial explanation. [Historical note and translated report](https://doi.org/10.32615/ps.2025.007).

## 7. A flash, then time to recover: Emerson and Arnold, 1932

**Story.** Short light flashes separated by dark intervals helped separate rapid photochemical events from slower subsequent processes. Their 1932 experiments underpinned the photosynthetic-unit concept: many pigments cooperate with a smaller number of photochemical centres.

**Possible animation.** Compare continuous light with flashes; add an antenna feeding a reaction centre, and show recovery between flashes.

**Accuracy check.** Do not use the historical chlorophyll-per-O₂ estimate as a fixed modern antenna size. “Reaction centre” and the detailed energy-transfer interpretation developed subsequently. [Intermittent-light paper](https://doi.org/10.1085/jgp.15.4.391), [historical account of the photosynthetic unit](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz171).

## 8. Chloroplasts release oxygen outside the intact cell: Hill, 1937

**Story.** Isolated chloroplasts evolved oxygen in light with suitable artificial electron acceptors. Hill followed oxygen using haemoglobin oxygenation. This made a partial reaction experimentally accessible without reproducing the whole carbon-assimilation pathway.

**Possible animation.** Replace the downstream acceptor with an artificial one, keep illumination on, and show oxygen production continuing.

**Accuracy check.** Hill's experiment and the later isotope evidence that evolved oxygen originates from water answer different questions. Neither is the discovery of ATP synthesis. [Hill's report](https://doi.org/10.1038/139881a0).

## 9. Two colours together do more: Emerson enhancement, 1957; Z scheme, 1960

**Story.** Red plus far-red illumination could produce a response greater than the sum obtained separately. Alongside other evidence, this supported two cooperating light reactions. Hill and Bendall's 1960 paper presented an influential working hypothesis connecting redox components.

**Possible animation.** Compare red alone, far-red alone, and both together, then reveal two light-driven energy boosts along electron transport.

**Accuracy check.** The Z scheme was built from several lines of evidence, not invented from one measurement. Treat its vertical axis as redox energy/potential, not the physical height of proteins. [Enhancement history](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz171), [Hill and Bendall](https://doi.org/10.1038/186136a0).

## 10. An oxygen-producing clock: Joliot, 1969; Kok and colleagues, 1970

**Story.** Oxygen yield oscillates with a period of four under a sequence of single-turnover flashes. The S-state model explains the accumulation of four oxidizing equivalents before O₂ formation. Modern time-resolved X-ray work now resolves intermediates during the final transition.

**Possible animation.** Advance the S-state counter once per successful PSII turnover; release O₂ during S₃ → [S₄] → S₀.

**Accuracy check.** A dark-adapted population often starts predominantly in S₁, giving an early oxygen maximum on the third flash. A four-step cycle does not require the first burst to occur on flash four. [Historical account](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz171), [Bhowmick et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06038-z).

## 11. Light makes ATP: Arnon and colleagues, 1954

**Story.** Work with isolated chloroplasts established photophosphorylation. Distinguishing ATP production from carbon assimilation opened the way to studying cyclic and non-cyclic electron flow separately.

**Possible animation.** Show ATP as an output before adding NADPH and carbon assimilation; later close the cyclic path around PSI.

**Accuracy check.** Do not compress all discoveries of ATP production, cyclic flow, and chemiosmotic coupling into one experiment or year. [Historical synthesis, “Photophosphorylation”](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcz171).

## 12. Energy stored across a membrane: Mitchell, 1961

**Story.** Mitchell proposed chemiosmotic coupling: electron transfer establishes an electrochemical proton gradient, and the return flow can drive phosphorylation. The proposal gave a membrane an active energetic role.

**Possible animation.** First show separated charges and proton concentrations; then allow proton return through ATP synthase.

**Accuracy check.** The proton motive force includes electrical potential and the chemical gradient. Mitchell's proposal and the later chloroplast experiment are separate evidence. [Original hypothesis](https://doi.org/10.1038/191144a0).

## 13. Making ATP in the dark: Jagendorf and Uribe, 1966

**Story.** Acid–base transitions in isolated spinach chloroplast preparations produced ATP without illumination during the ATP-forming step. An imposed pH difference supplied the driving force. This is a particularly clear test of chemiosmotic coupling.

**Possible animation.** Equilibrate with an acidic medium, rapidly transfer to alkaline medium with ADP and phosphate, retain acidity inside briefly, and show a transient ATP burst in darkness.

**Accuracy check.** “ATP in the dark” describes an artificially imposed gradient, not an indefinitely operating dark light-reaction chain. The original preparation and measured yield should not be replaced by an invented whole-leaf experiment. [Original experiment](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.55.1.170).

## 14. Watching a molecular motor turn: Noji and colleagues, 1997

**Story.** A fluorescent actin filament attached to the γ subunit made F₁ rotation large enough to observe by microscopy. ATP supplied to the isolated motor powered the rotation.

**Possible animation.** Start with the unusually long filament as a pointer, then zoom into the rotary catalytic mechanism. Connect it to the chloroplast motor only after identifying what the original experiment measured.

**Accuracy check.** This was F₁-ATPase driven by ATP hydrolysis, not a recording of a light-powered chloroplast synthesizing ATP. The reverse operating direction needs an explicit explanation. [Noji et al.](https://doi.org/10.1038/386299a0), [chloroplast structure, Hahn et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat4318).

## 15. Finding a photoprotection regulator: PsbS and NPQ

**Story.** Mutants deficient in PsbS helped establish its central role in rapid energy-dependent quenching. The field can identify crucial components before agreeing on every molecular quenching step. The 2025 NPQ review is useful precisely because it separates evidence from working models.

**Possible animation.** Compare a normal antenna with a PsbS-deficient one; acidify the lumen and show the different capacity to divert excitation into heat.

**Accuracy check.** Do not depict PsbS itself as a proven pigment-binding heat sink. Keep the PsbS response, xanthophyll conversion, and the physical quenching mechanism distinct. [Mechanistic review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmb.2023.168407), [2025 evidence synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1093/plcell/koaf240).

## 16. Which wavelengths count? McCree's crop measurements and the far-red update

**Story.** McCree separated action spectra, absorptance, and quantum yield in crop leaves. Later experiments show why a conventional waveband is not a hard biological boundary: far-red photons can contribute efficiently when supplied with shorter wavelengths.

**Possible animation.** Put absorbed photons and carbon gain on different axes; add far-red alongside shorter-wavelength light and compare with far-red alone.

**Accuracy check.** The 1971 paper is also frequently cited as 1972; retain its DOI to identify it unambiguously. Do not label 400–750 nm as an uncontested replacement for conventional PAR. [McCree](https://doi.org/10.1016/0002-1571(71)90022-7), [Zhen and Bugbee, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.13730).

## 17. Why a light-response curve levels off: Blackman, 1905

**Story.** Blackman's limiting-factor framework explains why increasing one input eventually stops increasing the overall rate. It is a useful conceptual ending after the machinery and energy budget have been established.

**Possible animation.** Increase photon input while downstream processing has finite capacity; plot assimilation rising and then approaching a plateau.

**Accuracy check.** A plateau does not mean photosynthesis stops. Keep this as the last teaching block and leave environmental-control and acclimation material to the other lecturer. [Blackman's paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aob.a089000).
