# Slides 4a, 4b, 6–14 rebuilt from the essentials (27 Oct 2026, for approval; nothing changed)

Each slide answers the question the previous one raises. The take-home is the slide's lead; the three points explain why or how, or give the evidence. Numbers only where they carry the idea.

| # | Question | Take-home (lead) | Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4a | What happens when a pigment absorbs a photon? | A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches a gap between electron levels; whatever its colour, the energy ends at the lowest excited state, S₁. | 1 **Absorption bands.** Red lifts an electron to S₁, blue to a higher level / Green matches only weak transitions, so it is absorbed less<br>2 **Internal conversion to S₁.** Higher states relax to S₁ within 1 ps; the excess becomes heat<br>3 **One S₁ excitation per photon.** Blue and red photons therefore drive the same photochemistry / Blue loses more of its energy as heat |
| 4b | Why do pigments absorb different colours, and why several pigments? | A pigment's structure sets which colours it absorbs; combining pigments lets a leaf absorb most visible light, green included. | 1 **Conjugation: longer chain, longer wavelength.** Alternating single and double bonds share their electrons / 3 double bonds absorb UV (≈ 286 nm), 11 absorb blue (≈ 450 nm)<br>2 **Accessory pigments: Chl b and carotenoids.** They absorb where Chl a absorbs little / and pass the energy on to Chl a<br>3 **Green light.** Leaves absorb 50–90 % of it / It penetrates deeper and drives photosynthesis in inner cells |
| 6 | Why so many pigments, and how does their energy get used? | Each reaction centre is fed by an antenna of hundreds of pigments; energy reaches it so fast that most absorbed photons drive photochemistry. | 1 **Antenna size.** In full sun one chlorophyll absorbs only ~10 photons s⁻¹ / Hundreds of pigments per reaction centre keep it busy / PSII: LHCII · PSI: LHCI<br>2 **Excitation energy transfer.** Energy, not an electron, moves between pigments 1–2 nm apart / Downhill: carotenoids and Chl b → Chl a<br>3 **Trapping: faster than fluorescence.** The reaction centre traps the energy within ~150 ps, fluorescence takes ns / So 84–91 % of photons absorbed by PSII drive charge separation |
| 7 | What does the reaction centre do with the energy? | The reaction centre turns the excitation into separated charge across the membrane: the step where light energy becomes chemical energy. | 1 **Charge separation.** Excited P680, the reaction-centre chlorophylls of PSII, gives an electron to pheophytin within picoseconds<br>2 **Stabilisation.** The electron moves on to Q_A, then Q_B, across the membrane / Each step is faster than the way back and gives up a little energy, so recombination is rare<br>3 **Oxidant and reductant.** P680⁺ (≈ +1.25 V) can oxidise water (slide 8) / Q_B, with two electrons and two H⁺, leaves as plastoquinol (slide 9) |
| 8 | Where do the electrons come from? | Water: the Mn₄CaO₅ cluster collects four oxidising steps, one per photon, then oxidises two water molecules at once, releasing O₂. | 1 **Four photons per O₂.** Each charge separation removes one electron from the cluster (S₀ → S₄) / At S₄: 2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻<br>2 **Evidence: flash O₂ yield.** O₂ peaks on every fourth flash, first on flash 3 (Joliot 1969; Kok 1970) / The cluster rests in S₁ in the dark<br>3 **Products.** The O₂ in the air comes from this reaction / The 4 H⁺ stay in the lumen (slide 10) |
| 9 | Where do the electrons go, and why two photosystems? | No single photosystem makes both an oxidant strong enough for water and a reductant strong enough for NADP⁺, so PSII and PSI work in series: the Z-scheme. | 1 **Two photosystems, two jobs.** PSII: strong oxidant, P680⁺ ≈ +1.25 V, for water / PSI: strong reductant, via ferredoxin (≈ −0.43 V), for NADP⁺ (−0.32 V)<br>2 **Evidence: Emerson enhancement effect.** Far-red light (> 685 nm) alone works poorly / Adding shorter-wavelength light raises the rate more than the sum of both (1957)<br>3 **Linking carriers.** Plastoquinol → cytochrome b₆f → plastocyanin → PSI / They connect PSII in the grana with PSI in the stroma lamellae |
| 10 | How is ATP made? | Electron transport stores energy as a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane; ATP synthase converts the backflow into ATP (chemiosmosis). | 1 **Proton gradient.** Water oxidation and the Q cycle at b₆f put 12 H⁺ into the lumen per O₂ / pH difference plus membrane voltage store the energy<br>2 **ATP synthase.** H⁺ flowing back to the stroma turn its rotor / 14 H⁺ per 3 ATP<br>3 **Evidence: Jagendorf acid-bath experiment.** Thylakoids moved from pH 4 to pH 8 made ATP in the dark (1966) / A proton gradient alone is enough (Mitchell) |
| 11 | Do the products match the demand? | The light reactions must deliver ATP and NADPH in the ratio the Calvin–Benson cycle uses and keep both photosystems balanced; alternative routes adjust both. | 1 **ATP : NADPH ratio.** Linear flow gives ≈ 2.6 ATP per 2 NADPH; the Calvin–Benson cycle needs 3<br>2 **Cyclic electron flow.** Electrons from PSI return to plastoquinone: H⁺ pumped, no NADPH / Without it plants grow poorly and lose PSI in fluctuating light<br>3 **State transitions.** When PSII is over-excited, part of LHCII moves to PSI |
| 12 | What if more light is absorbed than can be used? | In bright light leaves absorb more energy than photochemistry can use; the excess must leave as heat or fluorescence, or it forms damaging singlet oxygen. | 1 **Excess light.** Absorption rises with light; CO₂ fixation saturates / In full sun much of the absorbed energy is in excess<br>2 **Competing pathways.** Photochemistry, heat and fluorescence compete; their yields add up to 1 / When reaction centres are closed, heat and fluorescence rise<br>3 **Singlet oxygen.** Excitations that last too long form triplets, which turn O₂ into singlet O₂ / It damages PSII; carotenoids quench triplets in the antenna |
| 13 | How do plants protect themselves? | A low lumen pH, the signal of excess light, switches the antenna to dissipate energy as heat (NPQ); damaged PSII is repaired continuously. | 1 **Non-photochemical quenching (NPQ).** Low lumen pH activates PsbS and the violaxanthin → zeaxanthin conversion / The antenna then dissipates energy as heat<br>2 **Photoinhibition and repair.** PSII is damaged in proportion to light / The D1 protein is replaced continuously; damage shows when repair falls behind<br>3 **NPQ relaxation: a crop target.** After shading, NPQ relaxes over minutes and wastes light meanwhile / Faster relaxation raised field yield in tobacco and soybean, not in every crop or year |
| 14 | How do we measure all this in a living leaf? | Because fluorescence competes with photochemistry and heat, measuring it reveals PSII efficiency and NPQ without touching the leaf, from leaf clip to satellite. | 1 **Saturating pulse: Fv/Fm.** A pulse closes all PSII: fluorescence rises from Fo to Fm / Fv/Fm ≈ 0.83 in healthy dark-adapted leaves; lower under stress<br>2 **In light: ΦPSII and NPQ.** ΦPSII = (Fm′ − Fs)/Fm′: the share of light used by PSII / NPQ = Fm/Fm′ − 1: the heat dissipation<br>3 **Solar-induced fluorescence (SIF).** Satellites measure the same signal to track crop photosynthesis worldwide (e.g. ESA FLEX, 2026) |
