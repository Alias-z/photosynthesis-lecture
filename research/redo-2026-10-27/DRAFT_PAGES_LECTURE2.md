# Lecture 2 (carbon): take-home mechanisms (27 Oct 2026, draft; nothing changed)

Same style as slides 6–13: lead = take-home; points = mechanisms in plain words; numbers only where the number is the idea. 18 planned slides become 15: gas exchange C7 + C8 merged; refixation and C₂ moved into the C₄-evolution slide (Sage's steps).

| # | Title | Lead (take-home) | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | From light to carbon | In the stroma, the Calvin–Benson cycle uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to fix CO₂ into sugar. | **ATP and NADPH.** Made on the thylakoids, used in the stroma / Returned as ADP, Pᵢ and NADP⁺ to the light reactions | **Light activation.** Light switches on cycle enzymes via ferredoxin and thioredoxin / Rubisco activase prepares Rubisco, using ATP | **Photosynthetic induction.** After shade, CO₂ fixation takes minutes to reach full rate / Slow Rubisco activation costs crops carbon in fluctuating light |
| C2 | Carboxylation by Rubisco | Rubisco attaches CO₂ to the five-carbon sugar RuBP; the product splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). | **Discovery.** Algae given ¹⁴CO₂ for a few seconds carry the label first in 3-PGA (Calvin, Benson, Bassham) / Hence the name C₃ photosynthesis | **Rubisco.** A slow enzyme: a few CO₂ per second per active site / Leaves invest a large share of their protein and nitrogen in it | **Carboxylation.** RuBP + CO₂ → 2 × 3-PGA / This step uses no ATP or NADPH |
| C3 | Reduction and regeneration | ATP and NADPH reduce 3-PGA to triose phosphate; most triose phosphate is used to regenerate RuBP. | **Reduction.** 3-PGA → triose phosphate, using ATP and NADPH / Here the energy of the light reactions enters the sugar | **Regeneration.** Five of every six triose phosphates rebuild RuBP, using more ATP / Without RuBP regeneration the cycle stops | **Cost.** Per CO₂ fixed: 3 ATP + 2 NADPH / One triose phosphate leaves the cycle per 3 CO₂ fixed |
| C4 | Sucrose and starch | Triose phosphate becomes sucrose for export by day and starch for use at night. | **Sucrose.** Triose phosphate leaves the chloroplast in exchange for phosphate / In the cytosol it becomes sucrose, which the phloem carries to growing organs | **Starch.** Part of the carbon is stored as starch inside the chloroplast by day | **Starch degradation at night.** Starch is broken down at a steady rate, set by the circadian clock / It lasts until dawn, even when night comes early (Graf 2010) |
| C5 | Rubisco oxygenation | Rubisco also reacts with O₂; oxygenation produces 2-phosphoglycolate, which the cycle cannot use. | **Two substrates.** CO₂ and O₂ compete for the same active site / In air at 25 °C, about one oxygenation per three carboxylations | **Temperature.** In warm leaves, oxygenation rises relative to carboxylation | **Evolutionary origin.** Rubisco evolved when the air contained almost no O₂ / O₂ released by photosynthesis later made oxygenation unavoidable |
| C6 | Photorespiration | Photorespiration recycles 2-phosphoglycolate into 3-PGA, at a cost of CO₂, ammonia and energy. | **Three organelles.** Chloroplast → peroxisome → mitochondrion and back / In the mitochondrion, two glycine → serine + CO₂ + NH₃ | **Cost.** Of every 4 carbons, 3 return to the cycle and 1 is lost as CO₂ / Refixing NH₃ and recycling the carbon cost ATP and reductant | **Yield loss.** Photorespiration lowers the yield of C₃ crops, more in warm climates / C₃ leaves fix more CO₂ at 2 % than at 21 % O₂ |
| C7 | Measuring leaf gas exchange | An infrared gas analyser measures how much CO₂ a leaf takes up and how much water it loses. | **Infrared gas analysis.** CO₂ and H₂O absorb infrared light at different wavelengths; N₂ and O₂ do not / The absorption gives their concentrations | **Leaf chamber.** Air is measured before and after passing over the leaf / The CO₂ drop gives net CO₂ uptake (*A*); the H₂O rise gives transpiration (*E*) | **Stomatal conductance and *C*ᵢ.** Water loss and air humidity give stomatal conductance (*g*ₛ) / *A* and *g*ₛ give the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf (*C*ᵢ) |
| C8 | Light-response curve | As light increases, CO₂ uptake first rises, then levels off when the Calvin–Benson cycle limits it. | **Light-limited range.** In dim light, CO₂ uptake rises linearly with light / The slope is the quantum yield: CO₂ fixed per photon | **Light compensation point.** Photosynthesis equals respiration / Below it, the leaf releases CO₂ | **Light saturation.** At high light, Rubisco and RuBP regeneration limit uptake / Shade leaves saturate at lower light than sun leaves |
| C9 | CO₂-response curve and the FvCB model | The FvCB model explains the CO₂ response of photosynthesis: the slowest of three processes sets the rate. | **Rubisco-limited.** At low *C*ᵢ, uptake rises steeply with CO₂ / Set by Rubisco amount and activity (*V*cmax) | **RuBP regeneration-limited.** At higher *C*ᵢ, uptake rises slowly / Set by electron transport supplying ATP and NADPH (*J*max) | **TPU-limited.** At very high *C*ᵢ, uptake stops rising / Set by how fast triose phosphate is used for sucrose and starch |
| C10 | C₄ photosynthesis | C₄ plants concentrate CO₂ in bundle-sheath cells, where Rubisco works at high CO₂ and photorespiration is nearly absent. | **PEP carboxylase.** In mesophyll cells it fixes HCO₃⁻ into a four-carbon acid / It does not react with O₂ | **Kranz anatomy.** The C₄ acid moves to bundle-sheath cells around the veins / There it releases CO₂ next to Rubisco | **Costs and benefits.** Concentrating CO₂ costs extra ATP / It pays in hot, dry, bright conditions: maize, sugarcane, sorghum |
| C11 | Evolution of C₄ | C₄ evolved more than 60 times from C₃ ancestors, through steps that recapture photorespired CO₂. | **Refixation in C₃.** Chloroplasts cover the mitochondria / Part of the photorespired CO₂ is refixed before it escapes (Sage & Sage 2009) | **C₂ photosynthesis.** Glycine is decarboxylated mainly in the bundle sheath / CO₂ builds up there and is refixed by Rubisco | **When and where.** Mostly in the last 30 million years, as atmospheric CO₂ fell / In hot, dry, open habitats where photorespiration is high |
| C12 | CAM | CAM plants take up CO₂ at night and fix it by day behind closed stomata, saving water. | **Night.** Stomata open; PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ into malate / Malic acid is stored in the vacuole | **Day.** Stomata close; malate releases CO₂ / Rubisco fixes it, using ATP and NADPH from the light reactions | **Water saving.** Night air is cooler and more humid, so less water is lost per CO₂ / Pineapple, agave, cacti, many orchids |
| C13 | Rising CO₂ | Higher CO₂ raises photosynthesis and yield of C₃ crops, but lowers grain quality. | **C₃ crops.** More CO₂ speeds carboxylation and suppresses oxygenation / Field yield +18 % at ≈ 550 ppm (FACE; Ainsworth & Long 2021) | **C₄ crops.** Already saturated with CO₂ / They gain only in drought, by saving water through narrower stomata | **Grain quality.** Less protein, zinc and iron in wheat and rice grain (Myers 2014) |
| C14 | Improving photosynthesis | Crops use only a small fraction of sunlight; several steps of photosynthesis are now engineering targets. | **Faster NPQ relaxation.** Less light wasted after shading | **Photorespiratory bypass.** Glycolate is metabolised inside the chloroplast / Tobacco field biomass up to +24 % (South 2019) | **Calvin–Benson cycle and CO₂ supply.** More SBPase, faster Rubisco, C₄ rice / Field gains so far in tobacco (López-Calcagno 2020) |
| C15 | From leaf to loaf | The sugar made in the leaves fills the grain as starch. | **Sources of grain carbon.** Flag leaf and ear photosynthesis during grain filling / Stem reserves stored before flowering | **Phloem transport.** Sucrose moves from leaves and stem to the grain | **Grain starch.** The grain turns sucrose into starch, the carbon in bread |
