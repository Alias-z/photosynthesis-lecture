## L01 Photosynthesis

**1 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Sunlight supplies energy; CO₂ supplies the carbon for organic matter.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The opening establishes the two connected lectures.

### Agreed context unchanged

Source: `src/planning/opening.ts`. Previously approved on 22 October 2026.

#### Photo: capturing solar energy (45 minutes)

Pigments give photosynthetic organisms their colours and absorb light. The light reactions convert that absorbed energy into ATP and NADPH, which supply energy and reducing power for carbon assimilation.

#### Synthesis: building organic matter (45 minutes)

Plants use that energy and reducing power to convert CO₂ into carbohydrates. These support growth and food production: the starch in wheat grain, and ultimately our bread, contains carbon originally captured from the air.

#### The connection students should remember

Sunlight supplies the energy; CO₂ supplies the carbon. The two lectures explain how photosynthesis brings them together.

The colour of life and the bread that feeds us connect the two parts of the opening. ATP and NADPH can be named as the link here; their mechanisms come later.

## L02 Why is the sky blue, and why are leaves green?

**1 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** The colours we see reveal different interactions with sunlight.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** We have named sunlight as the energy source; now ask what happens when it meets air and leaves.

### Agreed context unchanged

Source: `src/planning/skyLeaf.ts`. Previously approved on 22 October 2026.

#### Different interactions with the same sunlight

The blue sky and a green leaf illustrate two different ways that matter changes the light reaching our eyes. Sunlight contains a broad range of wavelengths. The colour we perceive depends on which wavelengths reach us, their relative intensities, and our visual sensitivity.

#### Why air makes the sky appear blue

Sunlight’s oscillating electric field induces oscillations in the charges within air molecules. These produce radiation in other directions: light is scattered. For molecules much smaller than the wavelength, Rayleigh scattering applies, with scattering strength approximately proportional to 1/λ⁴.

Consequently, 450 nm blue light is scattered about 4.4 times more strongly than 650 nm red light under the same conditions. When we look away from the Sun, some of this scattered sunlight reaches our eyes, enriched in shorter wavelengths. Rayleigh scattering changes the light’s direction while essentially preserving its wavelength. It does not convert red photons into blue photons.

#### Blue rather than violet, and the different colours of sunset

Violet scatters even more strongly, but perceived colour depends on the entire scattered spectrum and the eye’s response. Our lower sensitivity to violet helps explain the blue appearance.

At sunset, sunlight travels through a longer atmospheric path. More short-wavelength light is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving the transmitted sunlight relatively enriched in red and orange. The observer’s viewing direction therefore matters: scattered skylight and the direct solar beam have different spectra.

#### Why a leaf appears green

Chlorophyll’s molecular structure gives it wavelength-dependent absorption bands, with strong absorption in the blue and red regions and weaker absorption in much of the green region. Absorption transfers photon energy into an electronic excitation of the pigment. The surrounding protein also influences these absorption properties.

#### Pigment chemistry and tissue structure

A whole leaf combines this selective absorption with reflection, transmission and internal scattering. Light changes direction at interfaces within the tissue, including those between hydrated cell walls and air spaces. Some light escapes back towards the observer. Because pigments remove blue and red light more strongly, the escaping visible light is relatively enriched in green. Leaf colour therefore reflects both pigment chemistry and tissue structure.

#### A green appearance does not mean most green light is rejected

A leaf can absorb substantial green light while reflecting more green than blue or red. Weaker absorption also allows green light to penetrate deeper before absorption. Under strong white light, this can deliver additional excitation to deeper chloroplasts whose photosynthetic capacity is less saturated than that of chloroplasts near the illuminated surface.

Terashima and colleagues demonstrated this advantage for additional green light in sunflower leaves; it depends on the background illumination.

## L03 The solar spectrum

**2 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Human vision and conventional PAR overlap, but they describe different biological functions.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** To understand the colours, locate visible light within the wider electromagnetic spectrum.

### Agreed context unchanged

Source: `src/planning/lightBasics.ts`. Previously approved on 22 October 2026.

#### Sunlight and human vision

Sunlight supplies both energy and information to life. It contains a broad range of wavelengths; a prism separates its visible components into a continuous spectrum from violet to red. Human eyes detect roughly 380–780 nm, using this light to recognise objects, colours and movement. This is the human visible range.

#### Photosynthetically active radiation

Plants capture energy from an overlapping part of the spectrum. Photosynthetically active radiation—PAR—is conventionally defined as 400–700 nm, encompassing blue, green and red light. Photosynthetic pigments absorb photons within this range to drive the light reactions. PAR gives us a common basis for measuring light available for photosynthesis; the amount actually absorbed depends on the pigments and the leaf.

#### Far-red light beyond conventional PAR

Biological activity continues beyond these boundaries. Far-red photons around 700–750 nm can support photosynthesis alongside shorter-wavelength light, illustrating why conventional PAR is a measurement range rather than a strict limit on photosynthesis.

#### Ultraviolet, infrared and microwaves

Toward shorter wavelengths, ultraviolet can trigger protective responses and damage cells at excessive exposure. Toward longer wavelengths, absorbed infrared contributes to warming tissues. Microwaves lie farther beyond infrared; their absorption by water and other molecules provides the familiar example of heating food. Different parts of the spectrum therefore interact with living material in different ways.

## L04 Photon energy and scattering

**3 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon and are scattered more strongly by air—two separate physical relationships.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The spectrum tells us which wavelengths are present; now distinguish what each photon carries from where light travels.

### Agreed context unchanged

Source: `src/planning/photonScattering.ts`. Previously approved on 22 October 2026.

#### Photon energy

Light carries energy in discrete packets called photons. Einstein’s 1905 light-quantum proposal connects each photon’s energy to its frequency: E = hν = hc/λ, where h is Planck’s constant, c is the speed of light and λ is wavelength. Shorter wavelengths therefore carry more energy per photon: a blue photon carries more than a red photon. Increasing light intensity at a fixed wavelength supplies more photons without changing each photon’s energy. This distinction matters when comparing the energy supplied by different colours.

#### Rayleigh scattering

Wavelength also affects how light is scattered. Light’s electric field makes charges within air molecules oscillate, producing radiation in other directions. Rayleigh scattering increases approximately as 1/λ⁴, so blue light is scattered much more strongly than red. This wavelength dependence comes from the molecules’ electromagnetic response; greater photon energy alone does not explain it. Scattering changes the photon’s direction while essentially preserving its wavelength.

#### The blue sky and sunset

When we look away from the Sun, this scattered light reaches our eyes and gives the sky its blue appearance. At sunset, sunlight travels through more atmosphere. More blue light is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving the sunlight reaching us relatively enriched in red and orange.

## L05 Why are leaves green?

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Pigments absorb wavelengths differently, so leaves return relatively more green light to our eyes.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The atmosphere explained the blue sky; selective pigment absorption explains leaf colour.

### Agreed context unchanged

Source: `src/planning/pigments.ts`. Previously approved on 22 October 2026.

#### Selective absorption and leaf colour

Light reaching a leaf can be absorbed, reflected or transmitted. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light strongly, but green light more weakly. Consequently, the reflected light is relatively enriched in green, giving leaves their familiar colour. Green appearance does not mean that most green light is reflected.

#### Different pigments, different absorption spectra

The pigments have different absorption spectra. Chlorophyll a and b have shifted blue and red absorption peaks, so their contributions overlap without being identical. Carotenoids absorb mainly in the blue to blue-green region, extending light capture. Peak positions also depend on the pigment’s molecular environment.

#### Green light inside a leaf

Green light still supports photosynthesis. Its weaker absorption lets it penetrate deeper into the leaf, while internal scattering lengthens its path and increases opportunities for absorption. Under strong white light, additional green light can reach deeper chloroplasts whose photosynthesis is less saturated, making it more effective than additional red light. Leaf anatomy and pigment distribution matter; thickness alone does not guarantee this advantage.

#### Accessory pigments in algae

Other photosynthetic organisms use different pigment combinations. In the red alga Griffithsia pacifica, phycoerythrin absorbs strongly around 540 and 565 nm, capturing green wavelengths that chlorophyll absorbs relatively weakly. These wavelengths are within conventional PAR: the contrast is between pigment absorption spectra.

## L06 Light-harvesting complexes

**3 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Complementary pigments form organised antennas that transfer excitation to reaction centres.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Pigment differences explain absorption. Protein organisation explains how that captured excitation becomes useful.

### Agreed context unchanged

Source: `src/planning/lightHarvesting.ts`. Previously approved on 22 October 2026.

#### Why chlorophyll a and b absorb differently

Chlorophyll a and b absorb different wavelengths because their molecular structures differ. Chlorophyll b has a formyl group (–CHO) where a has a methyl group (–CH₃). This changes the electron distribution and the energy gaps that photons must match. Surrounding protein charges also affect these gaps, so the same pigment can absorb differently in different protein environments.

#### Complementary absorption and different roles

Their absorption spectra complement each other. Chlorophyll b broadens light collection and generally transfers excitation to lower-energy chlorophyll a states. In land plants, chlorophyll a also supplies the principal donor pigments for reaction-centre photochemistry.

#### Organisation enables excitation transfer

To make these pigments work together, proteins organise them into light-harvesting complexes. The proteins control pigment spacing, orientation and excitation energies, allowing rapid transfer towards a reaction centre. During antenna transfer, excitation moves between pigments; there is no net electron transfer between them. Electron transfer and charge separation occur when the reaction centre performs photochemistry.

#### LHCII and LHCI in land plants

LHCII mainly supplies photosystem II and usually forms a trimer of three proteins. Each protein binds 8 chlorophyll a, 6 chlorophyll b and 4 carotenoids. Its chlorophyll a:b ratio is approximately 1.3.

LHCI supplies photosystem I and usually consists of four proteins beside the PSI core. The whole belt in one pea structure contains 45 chlorophyll a, 12 chlorophyll b and 13 carotenoids. Its chlorophyll a:b ratio is approximately 3.7–3.8.

LHCII is called the major antenna because its numerous copies bind a large fraction of plant chlorophyll. PSI also contains a substantial chlorophyll-a-rich core antenna.

#### What the pigment ratios imply

The pigment ratios have functional consequences. LHCII’s greater proportion of chlorophyll b strengthens complementary absorption. LHCI contains proportionally more a, including specially coupled pairs that create low-energy “red forms” absorbing beyond 700 nm. These arise from pigment arrangement and protein interactions, not from the ratio alone. Some require thermally assisted uphill transfer to reach the reaction centre: extending spectral capture can therefore slow trapping.

#### Two experiments, two discoveries

Two experiments connect this organisation to its discovery. Emerson and Arnold’s 1932 flash experiments helped establish that many chlorophyll molecules cooperate within a photosynthetic unit. Emerson enhancement in 1957 showed that red and far-red light together could produce more photosynthesis than the sum of their separate effects. The first supports collective light harvesting; the second helped reveal two cooperating light reactions, leading into how PSII and PSI drive electron transport together.

## L07 From excitation to charge separation

**3 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Reaction centres convert excitation energy into separated electrical charges.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The antennas deliver excitation to two kinds of photosystem. The next step changes the nature of the energy transfer.

### Draft lecture context

#### Two photosystems in one membrane

The light reactions take place in the thylakoid membrane. The aqueous space inside is the lumen; the space outside is the stroma, where carbon assimilation occurs. The membrane allows electron transport to establish a proton gradient between these two spaces.

Both PSII and PSI receive excitation from their antennas. Their reaction-centre chlorophyll a cofactors are embedded in different protein environments, giving them different electron-transfer properties. P680 and P700 are historical spectroscopic names associated with their donor systems, near 680 and 700 nm; they are not two entirely different chlorophyll molecules.

[Johnson (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-025-00847-y) · [Croce & van Amerongen (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay2058)

#### An excitation becomes a redox event

In an antenna, one pigment's excitation can be passed to another without a net electron moving along the antenna chain. In a reaction centre, an excited donor transfers an electron to an acceptor. The donor is left oxidised and the acceptor reduced: charge has been separated. Subsequent transfers help stabilise that separation instead of allowing immediate recombination.

To repeat the reaction, the oxidised donor must receive a replacement electron and the reduced acceptor must pass its electron onward. These requirements connect the two photosystems to the rest of metabolism. PSII ultimately draws replacement electrons from water; PSI receives them through the intersystem carriers and can reduce ferredoxin.

Both photosystems can be excited at the same time. Their cooperation is a connection between electron transfers, not a timetable in which PSI waits for all PSII reactions to finish.

[Johnson (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-025-00847-y) · [Hill & Bendall (1960)](https://doi.org/10.1038/186136a0)

### Proposed static study content

- Small membrane map: lumen | thylakoid membrane | stroma.
- Antenna: excitation transfer. Reaction centre: donor → acceptor electron transfer.
- Both photosystems absorb light; sustained operation needs donors and acceptors.

### Historical connection

Use: Recommended connection. Spoken allowance: 15 seconds, included in the slide time.

## L08 Water supplies electrons—and oxygen

**3 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** PSII extracts electrons from water; the released O₂ supports aerobic life.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** PSII can repeatedly separate charge only if its oxidised donor is reduced again.

### Draft lecture context

#### One reaction links photosynthesis to the oxygen we breathe

On the lumen side of PSII, the oxygen-evolving complex contains a manganese–calcium cluster that accumulates oxidising equivalents over successive photochemical turnovers. Through a redox-active tyrosine, it supplies electrons that replace those removed from the PSII donor system.

The net water-oxidation reaction is 2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻. Producing one O₂ therefore requires four successful PSII photochemical turnovers. The four protons are released into the lumen; the four electrons enter the transport chain. Water is not split simply because a free water molecule absorbs sunlight.

[Bhowmick et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06038-z) · [Murray JW. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.70925) · [Kok (1970)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-1097.1970.tb06017.x)

#### The oxygen comes from water

The O₂ released in oxygenic photosynthesis comes from water, not from the oxygen atoms of the incoming CO₂. Isotope experiments make this distinction visible: oxygen labelled in water can be traced into the evolved O₂.

That molecular by-product has a planetary consequence. Oxygenic photosynthesis supplies O₂ for aerobic respiration in animals, plants, fungi and many microbes. The same light-driven event provides electrons for reducing carbon and an oxidant that other organisms—and the plant itself—can use in respiration.

[Ruben (1941)](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja01848a512) · [Murray JW. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.70925)

- Four successful PSII turnovers per O₂.
- Evolved O₂ comes from water.

## L09 Two light-driven steps, one electron route

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Linear flow connects water to NADP⁺ through two cooperating photosystems.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Water supplies electrons at PSII. A second photochemical step at PSI makes them available for reducing power.

### Draft lecture context

#### Why the textbook route begins with PSII

The familiar sequence—water → PSII → plastoquinone → cytochrome b₆f → plastocyanin → PSI → ferredoxin → NADP⁺—describes net electron flow from a source to a sink. It begins at PSII because that is where electrons from water enter this route. It does not describe the order in which sunlight first reaches the photosystems.

Both photosystems absorb photons concurrently. PSII photochemistry makes water-derived electrons available to the intersystem chain. After passage through carriers, PSI uses a second excitation to reduce ferredoxin. Ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase, FNR, transfers reducing equivalents to NADP⁺: two electrons are required per NADPH.

[Johnson (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-025-00847-y) · [Hill & Bendall (1960)](https://doi.org/10.1038/186136a0)

The Z-scheme is an energy/redox view of this same membrane pathway. Its two light-driven boosts explain why red and far-red illumination can cooperate: the systems act in series in net electron transfer. The drawing is not a map of vertical positions in the chloroplast or a film of a single photon travelling from PSII to PSI.

For four electrons delivered from water to NADP⁺, the ideal minimum is four successful excitations in PSII and four in PSI. The outputs are one O₂ and two NADPH, while proton movements along the route also help establish the force for ATP synthesis. Actual leaves require more incident photons because not every photon is absorbed or produces productive photochemistry.

[Kramer & Evans (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.110.166652) · [Johnson (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-025-00847-y)

## L10 A proton gradient powers ATP synthesis

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Electron flow builds proton motive force; ATP synthase uses it to make ATP in the stroma.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The electron pathway supplies reductant; proton coupling explains its second energy output.

### Draft lecture context

#### Build a gradient, then use it

Water oxidation releases H⁺ into the lumen. Plastoquinone picks up electrons and stromal protons, and cytochrome b₆f processes quinol through its Q cycle, producing additional net proton transfer towards the lumen. A membrane that restricts free proton leakage allows this process to build proton motive force.

Proton motive force has both a chemical component, the pH difference, and an electrical component, the membrane potential. Protons returning from the lumen to the stroma through ATP synthase drive rotary catalysis of ADP + phosphate to ATP. The catalytic head faces the stroma, so ATP becomes available on the same side as NADPH and the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle.

[Johnson (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-025-00847-y) · [Hahn et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat4318)

#### One consistent ideal budget

For four electrons in linear flow, a full b₆f Q cycle gives a convenient account: 4 H⁺ from water oxidation plus 8 H⁺ transferred through PQ/b₆f accounting, making 12 lumen-side protons. The PQ proton contribution is already included; counting it again would overestimate the yield.

A plant chloroplast ATP synthase with a c₁₄ ring couples a full rotation to 14 translocated H⁺ and three ATP. Under ideal tight coupling, 12 H⁺ therefore support 12 × 3/14 ≈ 2.57 ATP, alongside two NADPH. This is a stoichiometric average under stated assumptions, not a promise that every leaf always makes exactly that ATP amount. Stromal H⁺ consumed in NADPH formation affects proton balance but is not another independently pumped batch of lumen protons.

[Hahn et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat4318) · [Kramer & Evans (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.110.166652)

### Proposed static study content

- H⁺ accumulates in the lumen; ATP is formed on the stromal side.
- pmf = a pH difference + an electrical potential.
- Illustrative budget per 4 e⁻: 12 H⁺ → approximately 2.57 ATP, using H⁺/ATP = 14/3.

### Historical connection

**ATP in the dark from an imposed pH difference** — Jagendorf and Uribe acidified chloroplast preparations, then imposed an acid-to-base transition with ADP and phosphate. ## L11 Electron routes make the system flexible

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Electron routing changes proton flow, reductant output and protection; its use depends on the state of the system.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Linear flow provides a reference ATP/reductant supply. Metabolism does not always demand those outputs in the same proportion.

### Draft lecture context

#### Three useful route families

Linear flow takes new electrons from water to NADP⁺. It produces NADPH and contributes proton motive force. Cyclic flow returns electrons from the PSI acceptor side towards the plastoquinone/b₆f pathway and back to PSI. It can support proton motive force without making net NADPH or evolving net O₂ through that cycle.

Oxygen-directed flow provides another outlet: electrons ultimately reduce O₂, so reducing equivalents are removed rather than retained as net NADPH. In a complete water–water cycle, gross O₂ production at PSII can be balanced by O₂ uptake downstream. Classical Mehler chemistry produces reactive oxygen intermediates that must be detoxified; flavodiiron proteins provide a distinct O₂-reduction route in several photosynthetic lineages. Native angiosperms lack the photosynthetic FLV system, so these routes must not be drawn as interchangeable universal leaf components.

[Walter & Kromdijk (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jipb.13206) · [Asada (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.arplant.50.1.601) · [Rizzetto N (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1111/ppl.70453)

#### Which route runs, and why?

There is no fixed ranking that the leaf follows in every condition. When NADP⁺ is regenerated by carbon metabolism, linear delivery to that sink can proceed. When downstream acceptors become reduced—for example during a rapid increase in illumination before metabolism catches up—proton regulation, alternative sinks and restricted electron delivery become important.

Cyclic flow recirculates electrons and can increase proton coupling without adding net reductant; it does not, by itself, dispose of a stock of excess electrons. An O₂-directed outlet does remove reducing equivalents. Their relative activities follow acceptor availability, redox state, enzyme regulation and proton motive force. Several routes can operate simultaneously.

[Walter & Kromdijk (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jipb.13206) · [Kramer & Evans (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.110.166652) · [Nikkanen L (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbabio.2026.149596)

#### Yield requires a route and an assumption

With a full b₆f Q cycle and H⁺/ATP = 14/3, the four-electron linear example gives eight productive photons, two NADPH and approximately 2.57 ATP. Four turns through a non-proton-pumping cyclic return require four PSI photons and supply eight translocated H⁺, corresponding ideally to approximately 1.71 ATP with no net NADPH. An NDH-mediated return can add proton pumping, so it has a different coupling account.

These are ideal yields per absorbed, productive photochemical event, not measured leaf quantum yields or percentages of solar energy captured. Metabolic ATP demand, ATP concentration and ATP production flux are also different quantities: recent evidence argues against claiming that every C₃ leaf must use one fixed cyclic fraction to fill an inevitable ATP deficit.

[Hahn et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat4318) · [Strand (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M116.770792) · [Degen GE (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41477-026-02374-w)

- Cyclic: electrons return to PSI; proton coupling without net NADPH.

- Route use depends on redox state and available sinks; no fixed universal priority.

## L12 Three fates of an excitation

**2 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Photochemistry, fluorescence and heat dissipation compete for excitation energy.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The electron routes described successful charge separation. Absorbed excitation can also leave by other pathways.

### Draft lecture context

#### Absorption does not guarantee photochemistry

After absorption and rapid relaxation within a pigment, excitation may reach a reaction centre and support charge separation. It may instead be lost by non-radiative relaxation, appearing as heat, or by emission of a fluorescence photon. In a leaf, chlorophyll fluorescence is predominantly red and far-red; some absorbed energy has already been lost before emission.

These pathways compete. If productive reaction centres become unavailable, excitation has less opportunity to drive photochemistry. Fluorescence can rise, but it need not: an increase in heat dissipation can lower fluorescence at the same time. A weak fluorescence signal therefore does not automatically mean efficient photosynthesis.

The key distinction is between the amount of light absorbed and the fraction used productively. The leaf cannot avoid every absorption event, but it can regulate the subsequent fate of excitation.

## L13 Too much excitation: protect the system

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Lumen acidification coordinates heat dissipation and electron-flow control to protect photosynthesis.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** The proton gradient stores usable energy and also signals when absorbed excitation is outpacing its use.

### Draft lecture context

#### A feedback from the lumen to the antenna

If light absorption exceeds the rate at which downstream chemistry can use its products, reaction centres and electron acceptors become increasingly constrained. Long-lived excited states and inappropriate electron transfers can generate reactive oxygen species and damage the photosynthetic apparatus.

A falling lumen pH helps activate the rapidly reversible, energy-dependent part of non-photochemical quenching, qE. Protonation changes the behaviour of PsbS and favours antenna states that dissipate excitation as heat. This uses the proton-gradient concept already established; it is not an unrelated protective switch.

[van Amerongen H (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/plcell/koaf240) · [Johnson (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-025-00847-y)

#### The xanthophyll cycle supports the response

Acidification also activates violaxanthin de-epoxidase on the lumen side, promoting conversion of violaxanthin through antheraxanthin to zeaxanthin. These pigment changes help establish and maintain protective quenching states. When excitation pressure falls, quenching can relax and zeaxanthin can be epoxidised back.

The central message is coordinated regulation: the same antenna that captures excitation can change its probability of dissipating it. Zeaxanthin is not simply a sunscreen that prevents all photons entering, and PsbS is not another chlorophyll antenna transferring captured light to the reaction centre.

Lumen acidification also restricts electron delivery through cytochrome b₆f, helping protect PSI when its acceptor side is constrained. Dissipating excitation and regulating electron flow act at different points in the same system. If damage nevertheless occurs, repair—such as replacement of damaged PSII D1 protein—is a separate response.

## L14 Reading photosynthesis through fluorescence

## C02 Following carbon into the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Rubisco adds CO₂ to a five-carbon acceptor, producing two three-carbon molecules.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** We know what supplies the energy. To discover the carbon route, follow a labelled carbon atom.

### Draft lecture context

#### A tracer reveals the entry point

Calvin, Benson, Bassham and colleagues supplied photosynthesising cells with radiolabelled CO₂, stopped the reactions after short intervals and separated labelled compounds. Comparing early and later labelling revealed a pathway rather than merely measuring total sugar production.

At the entry to the C₃ cycle, Rubisco adds CO₂ to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate, RuBP, a five-carbon acceptor. The transient six-carbon product yields two molecules of three-carbon 3-phosphoglycerate, 3-PGA. C₃ photosynthesis is named for this first stable fixation product, not for the eventual production of a three-carbon sugar.

Rubisco's carboxylation reaction does not itself directly consume ATP or NADPH. Their expenditure becomes clear when we follow reduction and regeneration.

## C03 Reduction and regeneration keep the cycle running

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Most cycle carbon regenerates the CO₂ acceptor; one net triose phosphate leaves per three CO₂ fixed.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Three CO₂ have entered a pool of eighteen carbons. Only part of that carbon can leave if fixation is to continue.

### Draft lecture context

#### ATP activates; NADPH reduces

For three CO₂ fixed, six molecules of 3-PGA are phosphorylated using six ATP and then reduced using six NADPH to form six triose phosphates. This is where reducing power from light-driven electron transfer changes the oxidation state of the carbon.

Triose phosphate is the useful three-carbon product pool. One net three-carbon equivalent can leave the cycle for biosynthesis; the remaining five triose-phosphate equivalents contain fifteen carbons.

[Sharkey TD. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/plphys/kiad601) · [von Caemmerer (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-025-04834-7)

Through rearrangements of carbon skeletons, those fifteen carbons regenerate three five-carbon RuBP molecules. This regeneration phase consumes another three ATP. The acceptor pool is restored and can fix the next three CO₂.

[Bassham (1954)](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja01636a012) · [Farquhar (1980)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00386231)

## C05 Rubisco reacts with CO₂—and O₂

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Rubisco oxygenation creates a carbon-recovery problem that plants must solve.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Rubisco connects incoming CO₂ with the carbon cycle, but its active site can also support an oxygenation reaction.

### Draft lecture context

#### Two reactions from the same five-carbon substrate

Rubisco carboxylation produces two 3-PGA molecules from RuBP and CO₂. Oxygenation instead produces one 3-PGA and one two-carbon 2-phosphoglycolate, 2-PG. That second product cannot simply proceed through the ordinary reduction phase of the cycle and can interfere with metabolism if it accumulates.

The competition depends on the CO₂ and O₂ available at Rubisco and the enzyme's catalytic properties. Increasing CO₂ around Rubisco can therefore reduce the relative frequency of oxygenation.

Oxygenation does not release CO₂ directly at the Rubisco step. Carbon loss occurs later during recovery of the two-carbon product. Keeping those steps separate makes photorespiration and the C₂ concentrating mechanism easier to understand.

[Bowes (1971)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-291X(71)90475-X) · [von Caemmerer (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-025-04834-7)

#### Avoid the 'hopeless enzyme' story

Rubisco combines difficult chemistry with finite turnover and imperfect CO₂/O₂ discrimination. It is useful to teach those constraints, but calling it simply a badly designed, unchanged enzyme obscures evolution. Comparative work finds continuing changes in catalytic properties in plants.

Plants can respond at more than one level: by recovering carbon after oxygenation, by changing where that recovery releases CO₂, and by concentrating CO₂ before Rubisco reacts. These alternatives connect carbon recovery with CO₂ concentration.

[Bouvier JW (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2321050121) · [Sage (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-arplant-042811-105511)

## C06 Photorespiration recovers carbon at a cost

**5 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Photorespiration salvages most of that carbon, while releasing CO₂ and requiring energy.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Rubisco oxygenation leaves a two-carbon product outside the normal carbon-reduction route.

### Draft lecture context

#### Follow carbon across three organelles

In a typical land-plant leaf, recovery involves the chloroplast, peroxisome and mitochondrion. Two molecules of 2-PG contain four carbons. After conversion through glycolate, glyoxylate and glycine, mitochondrial glycine decarboxylase and associated reactions convert two glycine molecules into serine, releasing one CO₂ and ammonia.

The three-carbon skeleton returns through glycerate to chloroplastic 3-PGA. In the canonical closed pathway, four carbons entering as two 2-PG are therefore recovered as three carbons in 3-PGA, with one carbon released as CO₂. This is a 25% loss from those two-carbon substrates, not a statement that every leaf always loses 25% of all photosynthesis.

Ammonia must be reassimilated and glycerate phosphorylated, so recovery consumes energy and reducing equivalents. Photorespiration is distinct from ordinary mitochondrial respiration and is active while photosynthesis proceeds.

[Walker B (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.14949) · [von Caemmerer (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-025-04834-7)

#### Salvage explains both its necessity and its cost

Without recovery, 2-PG would accumulate and carbon would be stranded outside the cycle. Photorespiration solves that problem, but it lowers net carbon gain relative to carboxylation alone. In the canonical account, each two oxygenations produce one photorespiratory CO₂ release: net carbon gain before other respiration is Vc − 0.5 Vo.

For a fully recycling carboxylation/oxygenation model, the combined demands can be written 3 Vc + 3.5 Vo ATP and 2 Vc + 2 Vo NADPH equivalents. These include restoring the carbon cycle after each reaction; they are not the cost of glycine decarboxylase alone. 'NADPH equivalents' is an electron account, not a claim that every organelle directly consumes NADPH.

Actual photorespiration is connected to wider metabolism: glycine, serine and one-carbon units can feed other pathways. That makes its physiological role richer than a pure waste loop, while also changing the canonical energy balance.

[Walker B (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.14949) · [Gashu K (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41477-025-02091-w) · [Walker & Schroeder (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-026-04961-9)

## C08 C₂ photosynthesis moves CO₂ release

**4 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** The C₂ glycine shuttle concentrates photorespiratory CO₂ near bundle-sheath Rubisco.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Anatomy can favour recapture. Relocating a key photorespiratory reaction makes that recapture more directed.

### Draft lecture context

#### Move the two-carbon carrier before releasing CO₂

In C₂ photosynthesis, mesophyll photorespiration produces glycine, but glycine decarboxylation is strongly concentrated in bundle-sheath mitochondria. Glycine therefore moves towards the bundle sheath before its carbon is released.

There, two glycine molecules are converted through glycine decarboxylase and serine hydroxymethyltransferase into serine, CO₂ and ammonia. The released CO₂ is close to bundle-sheath Rubisco and can be recaptured. Serine and associated metabolites connect back to the recovery network. The name C₂ refers to the two-carbon glycine shuttle, not to a replacement two-carbon first product of ordinary CO₂ fixation.

[Hylton (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00393064) · [Sage (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-arplant-042811-105511) · [Leung A (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.15069)

C₂ does not require the full high-flux PEPC/C₄-acid cycle that defines C₄ photosynthesis. It changes where an existing photorespiratory reaction releases CO₂, reducing the chance of that carbon escaping. It can lower the CO₂ compensation point relative to comparable C₃ leaves, though the magnitude varies among species and conditions.

Recent molecular work makes this evolutionary idea concrete: in Brassicaceae, loss of mesophyll expression can reveal an already-existing bundle-sheath regulatory programme for glycine decarboxylase. A useful innovation can arise by redeploying existing biochemical machinery.

[Dickinson PJ (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44319-025-00461-1) · [Leung A (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.15069)

## C09 C₄ photosynthesis concentrates CO₂ before Rubisco

**5 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** C₄ suppresses much oxygenation by concentrating CO₂, at an additional energy cost.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** C₂ concentrates CO₂ released by photorespiration. C₄ adds a high-flux concentrating cycle ahead of Rubisco.

### Draft lecture context

#### An extra cycle around the same carbon-reduction chemistry

In a typical two-cell C₄ leaf, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase, PEPC, fixes bicarbonate into a four-carbon product in mesophyll cells. Carbonic anhydrase connects incoming CO₂ with the bicarbonate substrate. C₄ acids such as malate or aspartate carry carbon towards the bundle sheath.

Decarboxylation releases CO₂ near Rubisco, raising its local CO₂ supply and reducing the relative frequency of oxygenation. The remaining three-carbon compound returns towards the mesophyll, where the CO₂ acceptor PEP is regenerated. The Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle still performs carbon reduction; the C₄ cycle supplies a concentrating system around it.

[Hatch & Slack (1966)](https://doi.org/10.1042/bj1010103) · [Ludwig M (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2023.07.013) · [Sage (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-arplant-042811-105511)

#### Concentration has a price

In the standard PPDK-based account, regenerating PEP consumes ATP to AMP, costing two high-energy phosphate equivalents per delivered CO₂. With no leakage and no photorespiratory cost, this adds approximately two ATP equivalents to the CBB baseline of three ATP and two NADPH per CO₂: an illustrative total of five ATP equivalents and two NADPH.

Real demand depends on leakage, pathway subtype and metabolite exchange between cell types. The ATP and reductant needs of the mesophyll and bundle sheath need not match each other. This reconnects directly to the flexible electron routes of lecture 1: proton-coupled cycling can matter especially in cells with high ATP demand.

C₄ is therefore not a replacement for Rubisco or a source of free extra carbon. It trades additional energy expenditure for a more favourable CO₂ environment around Rubisco.

[Yin X (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.14070) · [Nakamura A (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/plphys/kiag146) · [Ludwig M (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2023.07.013)

### Proposed static study content

- PEPC fixes HCO₃⁻ in mesophyll → C₄ acid → CO₂ released near bundle-sheath Rubisco.
- The CBB cycle remains the carbon-reduction pathway.
- Illustrative extra cost: 2 ATP equivalents per concentrated CO₂, before leakage.

## C11 What a leaf's CO₂ exchange measures

**3 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** Net assimilation is the balance of carbon gain and simultaneous carbon losses.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Carboxylation gains carbon, while photorespiration and other metabolism can release it at the same time.

### Draft lecture context

#### A chamber sees the net balance

A leaf gas-exchange chamber compares CO₂ entering and leaving at a known flow, with the necessary dilution corrections. This estimates the leaf's net CO₂ uptake; it does not directly count Rubisco turnovers.

For a conventional C₃ steady-state account, A = Vc − 0.5 Vo − Rᴸ: Rubisco carboxylation minus photorespiratory CO₂ release and other CO₂ release in the light. The 0.5 factor comes from the two-oxygenation carbon-recovery account. Rᴸ is often called day respiration, but it should not be defined as necessarily all, or only, mitochondrial respiration.

A net rate of zero does not imply that carboxylation has stopped. At a compensation point, gains and losses balance. This also explains why the light-response curve can cross zero.

[Farquhar (1980)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00386231) · [Sharkey TD (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.70216)

#### The enzyme experiences chloroplastic CO₂

CO₂ moves from ambient air, Ca, through stomata to intercellular airspaces, Ci, and then through mesophyll diffusion pathways to the chloroplast, Cc. A measured A–Ci curve is therefore not automatically a direct Rubisco response to Ci: the biochemical model's CO₂ substrate is Cc.

Diffusion and biochemical consumption jointly determine the CO₂ available to Rubisco.

[Yin X (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.14070)

### Proposed static study content

- A = Vc − 0.5 Vo − Rᴸ in the canonical C₃ account.
- Ca → Ci → Cc; Rubisco experiences Cc.
- Zero net exchange can coexist with active fixation and CO₂ release.

## C12 FvCB links carbon fixation to energy supply

**5 minutes · New proposals NOT YET APPROVED**

**Key message:** FvCB connects Rubisco capacity, RuBP regeneration and carbon loss in one biochemical model.

**Connection from the preceding slide:** Net exchange combines several fluxes. A useful model asks which process can support the lowest carboxylation capacity.

### Draft lecture context

#### Two capacities organise the first explanation

Rubisco can fix CO₂ only as fast as its catalytic capacity allows and only as fast as its acceptor, RuBP, is regenerated. In the basic FvCB picture, carboxylation is constrained by the smaller of these capacities. At low chloroplastic CO₂, the Rubisco-limited branch often controls the response; at higher CO₂ under a fixed light supply, regeneration can become limiting.

Vcmax represents a maximum Rubisco carboxylation capacity. J represents electron supply supporting the regeneration account, and Jmax is a capacity parameter for its light response. Subtracting photorespiratory and other CO₂ release gives the observable net assimilation A. This brings both lectures together: the light reactions supply energy, and carbon metabolism determines how it can be used.

[Farquhar (1980)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00386231) · [von Caemmerer (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-025-04834-7)

#### Keep the meaning of the quantities clear

For the usual reductant-based regeneration expression, Aj = J(Cc − Γ*)/[4(Cc + 2Γ*)] − Rᴸ. Cc is chloroplastic CO₂ and Γ* is the photorespiratory compensation point without Rᴸ. The denominator accounts for electron requirements of carboxylation and oxygenation; it is not simply an empirical curve shape.

J in that expression is not automatically the total PSII ETR estimated from fluorescence. Alternative sinks and different ATP/reductant coupling assumptions change the connection. That is why the flexible electron-routing discussion belongs before the model rather than being treated as an optional afterthought.

FvCB is a compact steady-state biochemical model, not a full simulation of induction, protection and every metabolic pathway. A further limitation can arise when the capacity to use triose phosphate constrains carbon metabolism; this is an extension of the basic two-capacity account.

[Yin X (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.14070) · [Lochocki & McGrath (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/insilicoplants/diaf014)

- Carboxylation is constrained by Rubisco capacity or RuBP-regeneration capacity.

- Net A includes photorespiratory loss and Rᴸ; Cc and Ci are not interchangeable.
